## Description
A navigation constellation broadcasts a signal that says, in effect, "this is satellite 14 and my atomic clock reads exactly this." A receiver that hears four of them solves four equations for three position coordinates and its own clock offset, which is why every GNSS receiver is also a precision timing device and why the timing product matters at least as much as the positioning one. Making that work needs rubidium and caesium clocks on orbit that drift by about a nanosecond a day, since light travels 30 cm in a nanosecond and clock error turns straight into ranging error, plus a ground segment that continuously measures each satellite's true orbit and clock error and uploads corrections, and enough satellites that four are always above the horizon everywhere. GPS runs 31 operational satellites in six planes at 20,200 km with an 11 h 58 m period, which puts each satellite over the same ground track twice a day; Galileo sits at 23,222 km, GLONASS at 19,100 km, and BeiDou's medium-orbit shell near 21,500 km. The signals are free and open, nobody is billed, and the result is the only piece of space infrastructure that most of the world's economy depends on without knowing it exists. The sensing-and-navigation sheet covers what happens on the receiver side, including jamming and spoofing; this entry is about the constellation and who pays for it.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Global coverage, sub-meter ranging accuracy, timing to tens of nanoseconds, and a marginal cost to the user of nothing make this the highest-leverage infrastructure per dollar in space. Medium orbit is what makes the fleet small: 24 to 31 satellites cover the planet where a low-orbit system would need hundreds. The weakness is power at the receiver. A signal transmitted from 20,200 km arrives far below the thermal noise floor and is recoverable only through spread-spectrum processing gain, so a few watts from a jammer denies it across tens of kilometers, and that is now routine over Ukraine, the Baltic, and parts of the Middle East. The second weakness is institutional. These are 15-year assets built on decade-long procurements, so capability arrives slowly: GPS III took the constellation from around $500M per satellite on the first units down to roughly $200M on the last, the tenth and final one launched in April 2026, and the first GPS IIIF has already slipped from February to November 2026 over a traveling-wave-tube amplifier. The ground segment has been slower still, which is the usual reason a new signal sits unusable on orbit for years after it launches.

## When to use
As a user you do not choose this, you choose how much to depend on it. The useful decision procedure is about the timing function, because that is where the exposure is largest and least visible: if a network, exchange, substation, or base station is taking time from GPS with no holdover, budget for a rubidium or chip-scale atomic clock that can free-run through an outage, and treat GNSS as the thing that disciplines it rather than the thing that provides it. If your platform operates anywhere contested, assume denial and design to inertial with GNSS as an aid. For a government deciding whether to build its own system, the honest test is sovereignty rather than performance, since GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou all work and a modern receiver uses all of them: you build your own when you cannot accept that another government could degrade or deny the signal, which is exactly the argument Europe used for Galileo. Low-orbit PNT is the interesting new option, but treat it as a resilience layer that supplements the medium-orbit systems rather than a replacement.

## Key numbers
31 operational GPS satellites in six planes at 20,200 km · 11 h 58 m orbital period · user range error under 1 m and timing accuracy of tens of nanoseconds · roughly $500M per satellite on the early GPS III units falling to about $200M on the last · 15-year design life · four global systems, plus a 258-satellite commercial low-orbit constellation planned against the 24 to 31 satellites a medium-orbit system needs.

## Regulatory and spectrum
Navigation spectrum is the most heavily protected in the civil radio spectrum, because a receiver looking for a signal below the noise floor has no defense against an interferer that a normal service would shrug off. The signals sit in radionavigation satellite service allocations near 1,559–1,610 MHz, with GPS L1 at 1575.42 MHz, L2 at 1227.60 MHz, and L5 at 1176.45 MHz inside an aeronautical radionavigation band reserved for safety-of-life use, which is why ICAO rather than any spectrum regulator effectively sets the terms for aviation. Coordination between systems is done by treaty rather than by license: the 2004 US–EU agreement on GPS and Galileo settled signal structures so the two would be compatible and interoperable rather than mutually interfering, and it is the reason Galileo's open signal sits where it does. None of these constellations is FCC-licensed; each is filed by its own administration at the ITU and its frequencies assigned nationally. Export control still binds the user side: military M-code and its predecessor keying are ITAR-controlled, and receiver exports remain restricted above the long-standing speed and altitude thresholds that keep GNSS out of foreign missiles. A commercial low-orbit PNT operator faces the hardest version of all this, because a signal existing receivers can already use has to live in the same protected bands as the government systems, and getting permission to transmit there is a slower problem than building the satellites.

## Examples
GPS, with 31 satellites operational and the final GPS III launched in April 2026 ahead of a GPS IIIF production run contracted to Lockheed Martin under options worth up to $7.2B; Galileo, run by the EU and EUSPA, now launching on Ariane 6 after the first Galileo mission on that vehicle in December 2025 and moving toward a second-generation fleet; GLONASS; and BeiDou, which adds geostationary and inclined geosynchronous satellites to its medium-orbit shell for extra regional performance. Regional systems include Japan's QZSS and India's NavIC. On the low-orbit side, Xona Space Systems launched its first Pulsar in-orbit validation satellite in June 2025 against a planned 258-satellite constellation, ESA is building an 11-satellite LEO-PNT demonstrator, and Iridium sells a timing and location service derived from its existing constellation.

## Economic profile
This is a public good with no direct revenue, and the economics only make sense at the level of the economy it enables. Operating and modernizing GPS costs the US well over a billion dollars a year across satellites, the ground segment, and the user equipment programs, and every other system is funded the same way, by a government that decided it needed one. The downstream market — chipsets, receivers, correction services, and the applications built on them — runs into the hundreds of billions of euros a year by EUSPA's accounting, and none of that money flows back to the constellation operators. The commercially interesting layer is resilience rather than positioning, because the free signal is good enough and only fails under attack. That is what the low-orbit entrants are selling: much stronger received power from 20 times closer, faster geometry change that resolves carrier-phase ambiguities in seconds instead of minutes, and authentication that makes spoofing detectable. Whether it becomes a business depends on whether aviation, autonomy, and critical infrastructure will pay a subscription for something they are used to getting free, which is the same question every backup-utility business faces.
