## Description
Optical coherence tomography is interferometry used as a depth-resolved imaging method. A broadband source has a short coherence length, so interference occurs only when the sample and reference path lengths match to within a few microns. Scanning that match point through the sample builds a depth profile, and scanning laterally builds a cross-sectional image. Modern systems work in the Fourier domain, either by dispersing the interference onto a line camera or by sweeping a laser's wavelength, which recovers the whole depth profile at once and is orders of magnitude faster than the original time-domain approach.

## Strengths and weaknesses
It produces micron-scale cross-sections of living tissue without contact, radiation, or contrast agent, which is why it became the standard of care in ophthalmology within a decade. Axial resolution comes from source bandwidth rather than from the lens, so it is decoupled from lateral resolution. The limit is depth. Scattering confines useful imaging to 1–3 mm in tissue, so it images the retina and the inside of an artery well and cannot see through a limb. Motion during a scan produces artifacts, and highly scattering tissue attenuates quickly.

## When to use
Use OCT when you need micron-resolution structure a millimeter or two below a surface without touching it: retinal imaging, intravascular imaging, dermatology, and non-destructive inspection of coatings, films, and layered materials. Use ultrasound when you need centimeters of depth and can accept coarser resolution. In industrial work, compare against confocal profilometry, which measures surfaces well but does not see subsurface layers the way OCT does.

## Key numbers
Axial resolution of 1–15 um, set by source bandwidth rather than by the objective · imaging depth 1–3 mm in scattering tissue · swept-source systems reach hundreds of thousands of depth scans per second · no contact, no ionizing radiation, no contrast agent · ophthalmic systems commonly $50,000–150,000.

## Examples
Retinal OCT in nearly every ophthalmology clinic, now the most common imaging procedure in the specialty; intravascular OCT guiding coronary stent placement; OCT-based inspection of pharmaceutical tablet coatings and multilayer films; art conservation imaging of varnish layers.

## Economic profile
Most of an OCT system is cheap by the standards of medical imaging. It works in the near-infrared and short-wave infrared, so the fiber, couplers, and detectors come out of the telecom supply chain at telecom prices, and the expensive items are the swept-source laser or the line camera. At $50,000–150,000 an ophthalmic system is a small fraction of what an MRI costs, and a clinic pays for it out of scan volume against a reimbursed procedure, which is why retinal OCT spread through the specialty within about a decade. Industrial OCT uses much the same hardware with none of that pull: the buyer is comparing it against confocal profilometry on a productivity case rather than a billing code, so the sales cycle is longer and the price ceiling is lower. Those are two different businesses that happen to share a physics, and when evaluating an OCT company the first thing to establish is which one it is in.
