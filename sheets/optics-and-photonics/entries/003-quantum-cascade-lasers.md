## Description
An ordinary diode laser emits a photon when an electron crosses the bandgap, which fixes the wavelength to the material. A quantum cascade laser instead uses transitions between engineered sub-bands inside a stack of quantum wells, so the wavelength is set by layer thickness rather than by chemistry. One electron cascades through 25–75 stages and emits a photon at each, which is where the gain comes from. The result is a semiconductor laser that reaches 3–12 um and beyond, where nothing else compact operates.

## Strengths and weaknesses
It opens the mid-infrared, which is where most molecules have their strongest absorption features, so a QCL plus a detector makes a gas sensor with parts-per-billion sensitivity in a package the size of a matchbox. Wavelength is designed rather than discovered, and tunable external-cavity versions sweep hundreds of wavenumbers. The costs are thermal and financial. Wall-plug efficiency is typically 10–20%, so most of the drive power becomes heat that a thermoelectric cooler must remove, and devices cost thousands to tens of thousands of dollars because volumes are small and the epitaxy is demanding.

## When to use
Use a QCL when you need a coherent mid-infrared source: trace gas detection, standoff chemical identification, infrared countermeasures, and spectroscopic imaging. It is the only practical laser for most of that band. Do not use it where a broadband source and a filter would do, since a thermal emitter costs a hundredth as much, and do not assume room-temperature continuous operation at the far end of the range, where devices often need pulsed drive or deep cooling. In the terahertz region QCLs work but generally require cryogenic cooling.

## Key numbers
Emits roughly 3–12 um in the mid-infrared and into the terahertz · one electron produces a photon at each of 25–75 cascade stages · wall-plug efficiency typically 10–20% · continuous room-temperature operation across much of the mid-infrared, with cooling needed at the extremes · unit cost commonly $5,000–30,000.

## Examples
Trace gas analyzers for methane, ammonia, and nitrous oxide; infrared countermeasure systems on aircraft; breath analysis and standoff explosives detection research; Alpes Lasers, Block Engineering, and Leonardo DRS as long-standing suppliers.

## Economic profile
The price sits in the epitaxy and in the volumes rather than in the materials. A device is a stack of 25 to 75 precisely grown stages, the yield on that is what it is, and nobody makes them by the million, so $5,000–30,000 is the normal range and the laser is usually the largest line item in the analyzer built around it. The consequence for anyone selling a QCL instrument is that the sensitivity has to be the product, because a thermal source and a filter cost a hundredth as much and answer most gas-detection questions well enough. So the market has mostly settled on regulatory emissions monitoring and defense countermeasures, where parts-per-billion sensitivity or a band nothing else reaches is the whole reason for the purchase. Wall-plug efficiency of 10–20% is a second-order cost, but it sets the cooler and the power budget, so it decides whether a product can be handheld and battery-powered. If QCL prices ever fall tenfold it will be because one high-volume application arrived, not because the epitaxy got cheaper on its own.
