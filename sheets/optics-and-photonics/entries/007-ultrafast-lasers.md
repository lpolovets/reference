## Description
An ultrafast laser emits pulses of picoseconds or femtoseconds by mode locking: forcing many longitudinal modes to oscillate with a fixed phase relationship so they interfere into a single short burst circulating in the cavity. A 100 fs pulse containing a microjoule reaches gigawatts of peak power while averaging only a watt. Because the pulse is shorter than the time heat takes to diffuse out of the illuminated volume, material is removed by ionization rather than melting, which is the whole point.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Cold ablation gives cuts with no recast layer, no burr, and almost no heat-affected zone, which is why ultrafast lasers do ophthalmic surgery, cut stents, and drill fuel injector nozzles. High peak intensity also drives nonlinear processes that no other source reaches, including multiphoton microscopy and attosecond generation. The costs are price and throughput. Systems run tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars, average power is modest so removal rates are low, and the pulses need dispersion management through every optic in the beam path.

## When to use
Use an ultrafast laser when thermal damage is unacceptable or when the process is inherently nonlinear. Transparent-material processing, medical device manufacturing, and multiphoton imaging are the durable applications. Do not use one where a nanosecond pulse would do, because you are paying a large premium for pulse duration you do not need. When comparing systems, compare average power and repetition rate rather than pulse duration alone, since throughput is set by how many pulses per second you can deliver, not by how short each one is.

## Key numbers
Pulses from about 10 fs to tens of ps · peak powers of megawatts to gigawatts at watt-level average power · pulse energy from nanojoules to millijoules · almost no heat-affected zone, which is what distinguishes the process · system cost commonly $50,000–500,000.

## Examples
Femtosecond lasers cutting corneal flaps in LASIK; stent and injector nozzle machining; multiphoton microscopy in neuroscience; the chirped pulse amplification technique that won the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physics and made high-energy ultrafast systems possible.
