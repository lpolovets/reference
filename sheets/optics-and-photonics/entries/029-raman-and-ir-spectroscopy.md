## Description
Both techniques read molecular vibrations, from opposite directions. Infrared spectroscopy shines broadband infrared through a sample and measures which frequencies are absorbed, which happens when a vibration changes the molecule's dipole moment. Raman illuminates with a single laser line and measures the tiny fraction of light scattered at shifted frequencies, which happens when a vibration changes the molecule's polarizability. The selection rules differ, so the two are complementary: symmetric bonds show up strongly in Raman and weakly in infrared, and vice versa.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Both give a molecular fingerprint without consuming the sample. Raman works through glass and plastic packaging and is almost blind to water, which makes it ideal for aqueous and biological samples and for identifying a substance inside a sealed container. Infrared is far more sensitive per photon and instruments are cheaper. The weaknesses are symmetric. Raman scattering is extraordinarily weak, roughly one photon in ten million, and fluorescence from the sample can bury the signal entirely. Infrared is defeated by water absorption and needs sample preparation or an attenuated-reflection accessory.

## When to use
Use Raman for aqueous samples, for measurement through packaging, and where no sample preparation is possible: pharmaceutical incoming inspection, hazardous material identification in the field, and polymorph screening. Use Fourier-transform infrared for routine organic identification, thin films, and anywhere the sample is dry and cheap instruments matter. If fluorescence swamps a Raman spectrum, move the excitation to 785 or 1,064 nm before concluding the technique does not work. For trace gases, a quantum cascade laser plus a long path beats both.

## Key numbers
Raman scattering yields roughly one shifted photon in 10^7 · common Raman excitation at 532, 633, 785, and 1,064 nm, with longer wavelengths reducing fluorescence · infrared fingerprint region 400–4,000 cm-1 · Raman is weakly sensitive to water, infrared strongly · handheld Raman units cost around $20,000–50,000, benchtop systems considerably more.

## Examples
Handheld Raman analyzers used by first responders to identify unknown substances through a bag; pharmaceutical raw material verification at goods receipt; Fourier-transform infrared as the standard organic identification tool in every analytical lab; Raman microscopy mapping composition across a sample.
