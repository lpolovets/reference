## Description
An optical coating is a stack of thin dielectric layers, each a fraction of a wavelength thick, deposited to control reflection through interference. Choose the thicknesses so reflections cancel and you get an antireflection coating; choose them to add and you get a high-reflector. The same tool makes bandpass filters, beamsplitters, and dichroics. Deposition method decides quality: ion beam sputtering gives the densest, most stable films and the lowest loss, while evaporation is cheaper and produces films that shift with humidity.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Coatings are what make multi-element optics possible at all. An uncoated glass surface loses 4% per face, so a ten-element lens would transmit under two thirds of the light; a good broadband coating brings that to a few tenths of a percent per surface. Laser mirrors above 99.99% and filters with steep edges both come from the same technique. The costs are environmental and mechanical. Coating performance depends on angle and polarization, films can absorb enough to matter at high power, and adhesion and abrasion resistance vary sharply with process.

## When to use
Specify a coating on every optical surface in any system where throughput or stray light matters, and specify it against the real angle of incidence rather than normal. Use ion beam sputtered coatings where stability, low loss, or high laser damage threshold is required, and evaporated coatings where cost dominates and conditions are benign. Ask for the laser damage threshold at your pulse duration and wavelength, because it is the specification most often quoted at conditions that do not match the application.

## Key numbers
An uncoated glass surface reflects about 4% per face · a good broadband antireflection coating leaves under 0.5% per surface, and a single-wavelength V-coat under 0.25% · high reflectors exceed 99.99% in a designed band · ion beam sputtered films are denser and more humidity-stable than evaporated ones · performance shifts with angle of incidence and polarization.

## Examples
Antireflection coatings on every camera and eyeglass lens; dielectric high reflectors in laser cavities; dichroic beamsplitters separating excitation from emission in fluorescence microscopes; the ultra-low-loss coatings developed for gravitational-wave detectors.
