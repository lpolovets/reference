## Description
A light-emitting diode is the same junction as a laser diode without a resonator: carriers recombine and emit photons spontaneously, in all directions and over a spectral width of tens of nanometers. White LEDs are blue emitters with a phosphor that converts part of the output. The blue gallium nitride emitter, which took decades to make efficient and won the 2014 Nobel Prize, is what turned LEDs from indicators into the dominant lighting technology.

## Strengths and weaknesses
Efficacy of 150–200 lumens per watt beats every other light source, lifetimes run tens of thousands of hours, and the devices switch in nanoseconds, which is why they double as sources for optical sensing and short-range communication. Cost is measured in cents. The limits matter when you want to do optics with them. LEDs emit into a wide angle from an extended area, so they cannot be collimated tightly or focused to a small spot, and their spectrum is far too broad for interferometry or narrow spectroscopy. Output falls with junction temperature, so thermal design decides real-world performance.

## When to use
Use LEDs whenever you need light rather than a beam: illumination, indicators, machine-vision lighting, fluorescence excitation with a filter, and UV curing. They are also the right choice for high-speed switching at low cost. Move to a laser diode when the light has to be collimated, focused to a small spot, or made narrowband. For fluorescence work, an LED plus a good excitation filter now competes well with a laser at a fraction of the cost.

## Key numbers
Efficacy 150–200 lm/W for white devices, against roughly 15 for incandescent · spectral width typically 20–40 nm · lifetime of tens of thousands of hours, defined as the point at which output falls to 70% · output falls measurably as junction temperature rises, so heatsinking sets real performance · unit cost in cents at volume.

## Examples
General lighting, which LEDs took over almost completely between 2010 and 2020; machine vision ring lights and bar illuminators; UV-C LEDs for disinfection; 365–405 nm arrays curing adhesives and resins in manufacturing.
