## Description
A fiber laser uses a length of glass fiber doped with a rare earth as the gain medium. Diodes pump the fiber through its cladding, the dopant absorbs that light and amplifies at its own wavelength, and fiber Bragg gratings written into the core form the mirrors. Ytterbium at 1,030–1,080 nm dominates industrial use; erbium at 1,550 nm serves telecom and eye-safe applications; thulium reaches 2 um. Because the gain medium is a long thin waveguide, the beam is defined by the fiber rather than by alignment.

## Strengths and weaknesses
The geometry is what makes it good: enormous surface area for cooling, a beam whose quality is set by the fiber core, and no free-space alignment to drift. Wall-plug efficiency of 30–40% is far above lamp-pumped systems, maintenance is minimal, and multi-kilowatt units are ordinary industrial equipment. The limits come from intensity in a small core. Nonlinear effects such as stimulated Raman and Brillouin scattering cap peak power and narrow linewidths, and transverse mode instability limits single-mode power. Delivering the beam through fiber also means back-reflection from shiny metal can damage the laser.

## When to use
Fiber lasers are the default for industrial cutting, welding, and marking of metals, and for anything needing a good beam at high average power with low maintenance. At 1,550 nm they are the standard for long-range eye-safe LiDAR and for directed-energy work. Choose a DPSS or disk laser instead when you need high pulse energy at low repetition rate, where nonlinearities in a fiber become the limit, or a wavelength ytterbium and erbium cannot reach. For thick-section cutting, compare against CO2, which still has an advantage on some non-metals.

## Key numbers
Industrial units from 100 W to over 100 kW, with 1–20 kW covering most cutting and welding · wall-plug efficiency 30–40% · near-diffraction-limited beam from single-mode fiber · pump diodes at 915 or 976 nm · maintenance intervals measured in tens of thousands of hours.

## Examples
IPG Photonics, which built the industrial fiber laser market; nLIGHT and Coherent in the same space; erbium-doped fiber amplifiers, the same technology that made long-haul optical communications possible; 1,550 nm fiber lasers in automotive LiDAR.

## Economic profile
Cost per kilowatt has fallen roughly tenfold since 2010, driven by pump diode prices and by Chinese entrants such as Raycus and Max Photonics taking a large share of the mid-power market. That decline moved fiber lasers from a premium option to the default for sheet metal cutting, and it displaced CO2 lasers from most metalworking. The remaining differentiation is at very high power and in beam-shaping for welding.
